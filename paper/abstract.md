# Abstract

The BENEFITS framework proposed a community-steward, edge-enforced architecture for participatory data sovereignty, but its own evaluation was necessarily qualitative: the privacy pipeline, budget accountant, and steward-quorum gate were still specification, and its trust result, a rise from 2.1 to 3.8 across 23 participants, measured responses to a concept rather than to built mechanisms. This paper closes that gap, building the full reference architecture on an Eclipse Dataspace Components connector and subjecting each sovereignty mechanism to direct empirical measurement across six experiments. The results are mixed rather than uniformly confirmatory. Enforcing the veto guarantee the predecessor specified, which the built system currently omits, substantially raises the measured quorum failure rate, revealing a real, previously undisclosed gap between the published design and the implementation. Signature verification, measured in a harness rather than the production consent filter, dominates cost from the smallest batch tested onward, with no crossover point. The steward-quorum gate, once built, proved to be a signature-count threshold rather than the cryptographic threshold-decryption envelope originally described, a real but weaker guarantee than specified. Exclusion error grows sharply with consent heterogeneity while ordinary differential-privacy noise does not, separating two previously conflated utility threats. Together these findings give the sovereignty claim a second, objective, mechanism-level line of evidence complementing rather than replacing the earlier subjective trust findings, establishing the technical readiness the announced three-country pilot requires.

**Keywords:** data sovereignty, Data Spaces, Eclipse Dataspace Components, quorum authorization, differential privacy, tamper-evident audit logs, mechanism-level verification
