# 6. Discussion

## 6.1 Two Lines of Evidence, Not One

SIDS 2026 (Katrakazas and Ntib, 2026) reported that participants who used a co-designed prototype trusted the idea of observable sovereignty controls: a self-report finding, drawn from a sample of 23 participants, that people responded positively to being shown what a system claimed it would do. That finding says something real about uptake and legitimacy, and nothing directly about whether the mechanisms those participants were shown actually behave the way the design says they do under load, under partition, or under an adversarial steward. The six experiments reported in Chapter 5 answer a different question: not whether people trust the architecture, but what the architecture, as built, actually guarantees, and at what cost.

It would overstate this paper's contribution to claim these two facts converge into a single, stronger proof of sovereignty. They do not measure the same thing, and a reviewer would be right to notice if the paper tried to make them look like they did. SIDS 2026's n=23 trust score is evidence that the promise of sovereignty controls is legible and credible to the people it is meant to serve. This paper's mechanism-level results are evidence that, independent of anyone's belief about them, the promised controls do what they claim under the conditions tested, sometimes at a cost participants were never shown a number for. The correct framing is complementary, not corroborating: two separate legs of one larger sovereignty argument, one subjective and participant-facing, one objective and mechanism-facing, neither one substituting for the other. A system could pass every experiment in Chapter 5 and still fail to earn trust in the field, for reasons the trust study is better positioned to capture than a wall-clock benchmark ever could. Equally, a system could earn high trust scores from participants shown a prototype and still leak revoked records under partition, refuse too many statistics to be useful, or grant a steward veto that nobody can actually exercise when it matters, none of which self-report can catch. Holding the two lines of evidence apart, rather than blending them, is itself the honest position this paper takes, and it is the same posture the project adopted when it corrected the steward-quorum claim discussed below rather than letting the original design description stand uncontested.

## 6.2 Returning to the Open Governance Challenges

SIDS 2026 closed with four open governance challenges that its own qualitative evaluation and design specification could name but not settle empirically, because the mechanisms behind them were not yet built. Chapter 5's experiments give three of those four challenges a real measurement to stand on; the fourth remains, honestly, outside what this paper measured.

**Privacy budget and community data utility.** SIDS 2026 stated a concern in general terms: differential privacy at $\varepsilon = 0.5$ per query and $\varepsilon_{\text{total}} = 2.0$ per site "can render aggregate statistics analytically meaningless at the low population volumes of individual service sites," and proposed exploring adaptive budgets and scenario-based community deliberation as a response, without a measurement to anchor the discussion. E4 supplies that measurement, at least for the epsilon and population ranges tested, and SIDS 2026's own operating point, $\varepsilon = 0.5$ per query, falls squarely inside the range E4 shows to be the steepest part of the accuracy curve: surviving median relative error falls most sharply between epsilon 0.1 and 0.5, before flattening toward a low floor from epsilon 1.0 upward, which means the exact budget SIDS 2026 proposed sits on the part of the curve where a small change in epsilon buys the largest change in usable accuracy. At the smallest population tested (30) and the tightest epsilon tested (0.1), surviving median relative error ranges from 0.513 to 1.127 across the four k thresholds, an error band that would make most service-level aggregates unusable for advocacy, exactly the failure mode SIDS 2026 worried about in the abstract. E4 also shows the concern is not uniform across the design space: at population 300, the same epsilon and k range starts substantially lower, 0.286 to 0.726, and both population sizes fall to a low floor, under 0.05 relative error at population 30 and under 0.015 at population 300, once epsilon rises to 2.0, though the population-30 floor rests on as few as 3 surviving data points out of 68 at some k values and none at all for k = 10, so it should be read as illustrative rather than a stable estimate (section 5.4 gives the exact counts). Suppression tells the same story from the other side: at population 30, k = 10, the suppression/refusal rate is 95.6% at epsilon = 0.1, rising to 100% by epsilon = 1.5, meaning most or all query attempts return nothing at all at exactly the small-site, tight-budget combination SIDS 2026 flagged.

G2 adds a second utility threat SIDS 2026's own framing did not anticipate, because it concerns consent heterogeneity rather than the epsilon budget itself. Holding epsilon fixed at 1.0, surviving exclusion error, which captures noise and exclusion bias together against the full-population ground truth, rises roughly seventeen-fold across the heterogeneity sweep, from 0.020 at h = 0.0 to 0.348 at h = 1.0, while surviving coverage error, which isolates the privacy mechanism's own noise and suppression behavior against the consenting population alone, stays flat over the same range, 0.020 to 0.050, with no comparable trend; since exclusion error nests coverage error's noise component, it is the widening gap between the two, not exclusion error alone, that is attributable specifically to heterogeneous consent. The excluded fraction itself, the direct share of the population lacking a valid grant for at least one query attribute, rises monotonically with heterogeneity independent of epsilon, reaching 1.0 at h = 1.0. Read together with E4, this means the "aggregate too thin to be useful" worry SIDS 2026 raised has two distinct causes in the built system: privacy noise and suppression at low epsilon and small population (E4), and exclusion bias from heterogeneous consent grants that grows independently of epsilon (G2). A community deliberation process built only around explaining epsilon, as SIDS 2026's adaptive-budget proposal frames it, would not surface the second mechanism at all; consent heterogeneity, not the privacy budget, is what drives it.

Rogers et al.'s (2021) LinkedIn budget accountant and Abowd et al.'s (2022) Census TopDown system are both real, deployed formal-privacy budgets with documented accuracy costs, but at population scales far above anything E4 tests: LinkedIn's audience-engagement queries and the U.S. Census both operate over populations many orders of magnitude larger than the 30-to-300 range that spans an individual service site. E4 sits in the gap between those two deployment reports and the small-population, single-site regime SIDS 2026's own governance challenge is actually about, and its numbers speak to a scale neither of those two systems' published results directly cover.

**Sustainable steward governance and sovereignty vs. institutional accountability.** SIDS 2026 named this tension directly: "A steward veto on outcomes data deprives institutions of needed monitoring information; conversely, embedding stewards in institutional structures compromises their independence." G1 gives the first half of that sentence a number. Arm B in G1 is not a hypothetical stricter policy invented for comparison; it is SIDS 2026's own specified steward gate, "any single steward may veto, in which case the release request is returned to the requester with a written justification" (Katrakazas and Ntib, 2026), the same guarantee section 3.4 shows the built system's Arm A never checks. G1 therefore measures the availability cost of actually enforcing the veto SIDS 2026 already promised, not the cost of a stricter alternative the design considered and rejected. At the filtered slice that shows the clearest separation between the two, veto_share = 0.3, N = 20, enforcing the veto (Arm B) raises the quorum failure rate far above the built system's current, veto-blind rate (Arm A) at every one of the 15 plotted combinations of quorum fraction and action probability, with the gap never smaller than 0.555 and reaching as high as 0.995. At quorum_fraction = 0.3, Arm A never fails across the tested range, while Arm B fails 91.0 to 99.5% of the time. Decomposing Arm B's failures shows this cost is not genuine quorum starvation: at every one of the 15 plotted points its starved rate is 0.0, and its entire failure rate is vetoed rate, a direct, mechanical consequence of a 0.3 veto share across a pool of 20 stewards, not evidence that a veto-enforcing policy stalls independently of stewards' actual objections. This does not settle the normative question SIDS 2026 raised, how much institutional monitoring access is worth how much steward leverage, which remains a governance choice rather than a technical one, but it gives the people who have to make that choice, including the three-country pilot's own comparison of stipend, in-kind, and institutional-embedding sustainability models, a real number for what enforcing the already-published veto guarantee would cost, rather than the false reassurance that the currently unenforced policy already represents a validated design choice. It also bears on sustainable governance in a narrower sense: a fully enforced veto policy gives stewards, particularly stipend-supported or part-time ones, a real blocked-release backlog to manage once vetoes actually stop a release, a workload cost the pilot's stipend-model comparison did not previously have a mechanism-level number to weigh against.

**Multi-channel sovereignty equivalence.** SIDS 2026's liaison-accountability protocol for paper-form submissions, SMS-confirmed digitization acknowledgment, liaison-level audit logging, and participant-accessible retention of paper originals, has no corresponding experiment in this paper. None of the six experiments touch the liaison-digitization path, and no component of the built system described in Chapter 3 implements or simulates it. The audit-chain experiments (E3) measure proof size and verification time for entries already in the system, which says nothing about whether a liaison's transcription of a paper form into that system preserved the participant's actual intent at the point of digitization. Closing this gap would require a different kind of study, one centered on the liaison interaction itself, not a mechanism-level benchmark of the kind this paper runs. It remains open, and this chapter does not force a connection where none exists.

## 6.3 The Steward-Quorum Gaps and What "Verified, Not Architected" Requires

Section 3.4 already states the central correction this paper has to make about its own system: SIDS 2026 described the steward-release gate as a cryptographic envelope, aggregated outputs encrypted under a key reconstructable only from a quorum of steward signatures, and the design specification that followed initially retained Shamir secret sharing as a second lock on release precisely because a policy check the connector operator runs is a weaker guarantee than a cryptographic impossibility. What was actually built and verified against a running connector is `StewardQuorumFunction`, an ODRL constraint function that counts distinct steward signatures on a `StewardAuthorization` record and refuses contract negotiation unless that count meets a threshold. It is a real, negotiation-blocking gate, not a decorative one, but it is enforced by the software the connector operator runs, not by mathematics that holds regardless of who operates that software.

A second gap sits alongside the cryptographic one, and it is the more serious of the two. Section 3.4 discloses it directly: SIDS 2026's own text specifies not just a quorum but a veto, "any single steward may veto, in which case the release request is returned to the requester with a written justification" (Katrakazas and Ntib, 2026), and `StewardQuorumFunction.evaluate` never reads `authorization.vetoes()`; a recorded veto has no effect on whether a release clears contract negotiation. This gap is more serious than the cryptographic one not because it costs more in absolute terms, but because it diverges from a guarantee SIDS 2026 already published to its readers, rather than from an internal design intent that a reference implementation stopped short of realizing. G1 (section 5.5) gives this gap a measured cost rather than leaving it a qualitative disclosure: at the slice with the clearest separation, enforcing the veto SIDS 2026 specified would raise the quorum failure rate by at least 0.555 and as much as 0.995 above what the built system's veto-blind policy currently exhibits, and that cost is driven almost entirely by vetoes firing as designed rather than by genuine quorum starvation, since Arm B's starved rate is 0.0 at every plotted point in that slice. The steward-quorum limitation this paper discloses is therefore two gaps, not one: a cryptographic-envelope gap between the policy-engine check that was built and the threshold-decryption envelope SIDS 2026 described, and a veto-checking gap between the guarantee SIDS 2026 published and what the release decision actually consults.

This chapter's job is to say what these gaps mean, beyond restating that they exist. A paper whose thesis is that mechanism-level measurement should replace unverified architectural claims cannot itself carry an unverified architectural claim about one of its own central mechanisms. If this paper described the steward gate as cryptographic threshold decryption while the code implements a signature-count check, it would be doing exactly what SIDS 2026's own "Technical Implementation Status" section was careful not to do: describing a design as though it were the built system, rather than naming the gap between the two. The honest path, taken here and in Section 3.4, is to report the mechanism for what it is: real, load-bearing, and weaker than originally specified, rather than smoothing the distance between the two into a shared vocabulary that makes them sound equivalent. Albalwy (2026) offers a useful contrast from a comparable domain: ZK-EHR replaces an asserted governance property (a requester's policy compliance) with one verified through zero-knowledge proofs, checkable by any party without trusting the operator who runs the check. That is the general move this paper's own thesis argues for, measuring what a system actually guarantees rather than what it claims to. The cryptographic-envelope gap is the place in this reference implementation where that move has not yet been completed: the quorum guarantee is checked, but only by the party operating the check, not proven to an independent verifier the way Albalwy's zero-knowledge approach or Kokoris-Kogias et al.'s (2020) CALYPSO threshold decryption would allow. The veto-checking gap is a different kind of incompleteness, not a missing independence property but a missing check: the mechanism to honor SIDS 2026's promised veto already exists in `StewardAuthorization`'s `vetoes` list, and simply is not consulted.

Closing the cryptographic gap is a concrete, scoped engineering task, not a redesign. Section 3.4 already sets out the two closest comparators, CALYPSO's genuine threshold decryption benchmarked against committee size and Raisaro et al.'s (2019) MedCo, where decryption requires collective participation from every federated site holding a key share; either pattern, applied here, would replace the current signature-count check with a real threshold-decryption step, `ReleaseArtifact` bytes encrypted under a key that only a quorum of stewards can jointly reconstruct, so that no connector operator, however configured, can produce plaintext without that quorum's actual cryptographic participation. The auditable structure this implementation already has, signed authorizations bound to a specific dataset hash, with separate review, veto, and appeal records, is compatible with that upgrade; it would sit alongside the cryptographic gate rather than being replaced by it. What is missing is the independence of the guarantee from the software making the check, which is precisely the property CALYPSO and MedCo hold and this implementation does not yet.

## 6.4 E1 and the Absence of a Crossover

Chapter 5 reported E1's result carefully: across every field count and every batch size swept, from 10 documents to 10,000, signature verification is the costliest of the three arms and structural checking the second costliest, with no crossover anywhere in the data. E1's signature arm runs only inside its own harness, against a separate CouchDB design document E1 builds and installs itself, not against the production `consent-couchdb` design document any real deployment provisions; the production S1 filter performs no per-record signature verification at all, so E1 measures what per-record signature verification would cost if it were added to the write path, not a cost the deployed system currently pays. That measured cost is worth stating plainly on its own terms: a fixed per-signature cost might, in principle, eventually be outrun by a cost that scales worse with batch size, producing a crossover point past which structural or baseline validation becomes the bottleneck instead. The measured data show no such crossover anywhere in the swept range. The signature-to-structural ratio stays within a 50-to-104-fold band across the entire swept range, and the signature-to-baseline ratio within a 112-to-460-fold band; neither ratio drifts toward parity as batch size grows, because the pure-JavaScript Ed25519 implementation E1's harness measures scales with the number of documents at roughly the same rate the other arms do, just with a much larger constant factor per document.

The practical implication is specific rather than general: if signature verification of this kind were added to the device-tier write path at small batch sizes, its cost should not be expected to matter only "at scale." At field_count = 1 and the smallest batch tested (10 documents), the harness measures signature verification alone already costing 682.41 ms, against 6.07 ms for baseline and 10.85 ms for structural, and it stays the dominant arm at every larger batch size measured. A future design that assumed signature checking could be deferred or batched away until some volume threshold, on the reasoning that structural validation would become the bottleneck first, would be building on an assumption this data does not support. The correct reading of E1 is not that signature verification is disqualifying, the harness shows the architecture would still function correctly under it, but that its cost, were it added, has to be budgeted for from the first document onward, not held in reserve as a concern for a later scaling stage.

## 6.5 Limitations

The limitations of this paper are the direct consequence of its own scope, and they are worth stating without softening. All six experiments in Chapter 5 are mechanism-level and simulation-based: E1 measures wall-clock time in a controlled harness, E2 and G1 simulate device fleets and steward pools under seeded stochastic models rather than observing real devices or real stewards, E3 times and sizes calls into audit-chain logic without deploying it against live traffic, and E4 and G2 run synthetic populations through the privacy pipeline rather than real service-site data. None of the six is a field measurement, and none involves a real participant, a real steward, or a real service provider. That is by design, the pilot SIDS 2026 already announced across London, Tuscany, and Zaragoza is the intended venue for field-conditions evidence, and this paper's own thesis is explicitly scoped as the technical readiness gate that precedes it, not a substitute for it. But it means every number in Chapter 5 describes how the built mechanisms behave under the conditions this project's own harnesses constructed, and those conditions, however carefully seeded and swept, are not the same as three years of real deployment across three legal jurisdictions.

The results are also single-implementation results. There is one EDC connector configuration, one CouchDB validation function, one Merkle-epoch audit chain, one privacy pipeline, and one quorum-policy implementation, each measured against itself, not against an alternative reference implementation solving the same problem a different way. The comparators discussed in Section 3.4 and above, CALYPSO, MedCo, Albalwy's ZK-EHR, are structurally close but were not run head-to-head against this system on the same workload; the comparison in this chapter is qualitative, drawn from their own published numbers, not a controlled benchmark this paper conducted. A different EDC deployment, a different audit-chain epoch strategy, or a different DP accounting scheme might show a different cost profile, and this paper cannot rule that out from a single build.

A further limitation is the audit chain's coverage boundary, which Chapter 3 (section 3.6) states is load-bearing for what this paper can claim about the audit trail. `benefits-audit` subscribes to connector events, but EDC 0.18.0 rejects a policy-violating negotiation offer synchronously, inside its Dataspace Protocol handler, before any `ContractNegotiation` entity is ever created, so no event fires and no audit entry is written for that class of refusal, confirmed empirically against a running provider. The audit chain therefore covers every negotiation admitted into the provider's state machine and every transfer attempt, but not the policy-refused negotiations, missing steward quorum, purpose mismatch, insufficient k, or an exhausted privacy budget among them, that the steward-quorum, k-anonymity, and epsilon-budget machinery exist specifically to produce. This is not a gap this paper's experiments close; E3 measures the cost of verifying entries that were written, not whether every event that should have produced an entry did.

A second limitation concerns when consent scoping is actually checked. Neither of the two enforcement points this paper measures, the S1 evaluator at the device boundary and the S2 constraint functions at the connector boundary, originally gated whether an already-written contribution could be included in a query-time aggregate; `Contribution`, the record type the privacy pipeline operates on, carried no consent information at all, so an aggregate result could include a contribution regardless of the consent scope its owner had granted, once that contribution had already been written. This gap went unaddressed until building the G2 experiment required closing it, at which point query-time consent scoping was added to the production `PrivacyPipeline` itself rather than confined to the experiment harness. That the gap was found only because an experiment needed it closed, not because a review of the enforcement points caught it first, is worth stating plainly: it leaves open the possibility that other query-time or aggregate-time gaps of the same shape exist and have not yet been found by an experiment that happens to need them closed.

A third limitation concerns identity. This deployment uses EDC's mock identity module rather than IdentityHub with decentralized identifiers and verifiable credentials, deferred on the argument that every claim in this paper concerns what happens after a participant is already identified, not how that identification is established. That argument holds for the consent and privacy mechanisms this paper measures, but not fully for the steward-quorum guarantee: a quorum of signatures is only as meaningful as the independence of the parties signing, and independence depends on stewards being distinct, independently-authenticated parties, which a shared mock identity module does not, by itself, guarantee. This deferral is therefore not fully orthogonal to the sovereignty claims this paper measures, and it is stated here as an honestly-owned limitation rather than one this paper's scope allows it to resolve.

Finally, the steward-quorum gaps described in Section 6.3, the cryptographic-envelope gap and the veto-checking gap, are themselves limitations of the built system, not only of what this paper claims about it. The signature-count check is real and negotiation-blocking, but it is not independent of the connector operator the way the original design specification, and SIDS 2026's own description, intended; and a steward's recorded veto has no effect on the release decision, even though SIDS 2026's own published text specifies that it should. A community whose sovereignty depends on this gate is currently depending on the correct behavior of the software the connector operator runs, and on vetoes being honored by a mechanism that does not yet honor them. Section 6.3 above states what closing each gap would require; until they are closed, the honest description of the steward-quorum mechanism is the one given there and in Section 3.4, not the stronger one SIDS 2026's original design language would suggest.
